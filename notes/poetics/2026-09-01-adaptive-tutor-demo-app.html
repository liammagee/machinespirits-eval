The tutor that knows why you're stuck
§ Machine Spirits · a guided walk-through · updated 2026-09-01

The right move at the stuck moment

A tutoring system built to name the kind of difficulty a learner is in, choose a move to match, and record what it said so the choice can be checked. What has been shown is the second step: given the moment, the matched move helps. Finding the moment on its own depends on what the reading is taken from. Readings taken from the proof itself carry to new scenarios; readings taken from the learner's words are shown only on the scenarios the detector was built from. This page explains the approach and lets you read three scenarios, each tutored twice.

In these counts the experimenter chose the moment and the card; the tutor wrote the reply. The live detector that would choose for itself is tested separately, and it has only been shown to work on the scenarios its word lists came from.

01·

Start here

What this project is, and what problem it works on.

Tutoring dialogues have moments where the next reply matters more than any other: a learner presses for the answer before the work is done, jokes to deflect, feels their effort has been dismissed, misremembers what was shown, or refuses a conclusion because accepting it would cost them face. A good human tutor recognises which of these is happening and responds to that, not to the surface words.

Language-model tutors are fluent explainers, and at these moments explaining is usually the wrong move: it answers a question the learner has already stopped asking. This project treats adaptation as a decision the system makes in the open. Before each reply it classifies the learner's state, selects a concrete move for that state from a small, tested set, and keeps the detected state, the chosen move, the delivered words and the learner's next turn as separate records. That makes the decision inspectable, and it makes it testable: the same moment can be replayed with a deliberately wrong move to see whether the right one was doing anything.

Read the state, not the sentence.

Two kinds of state are read before the tutor writes. One is where the learner stands in the proof: a step taken without its warrant, a run of turns that only ask permission, a clue that has come due. That reading comes from the proof structure every scenario has, so it carries. The other is how the learner is taking it: deadline demand, mockery, grievance, false memory, a fused social stake, confusion, switched-off flatness. That reading is today a set of word lists compiled from two scenarios, and it goes quiet on a new one. The ledger keeps the two apart.

Choose a move that fits.

Each state has a short instruction card naming one move: accept the deadline and narrow the check; keep the content but change the register; credit what the learner has already done before correcting; reopen the record rather than assert; separate the finding from what it costs the learner to accept it.

Keep the records apart.

The draft, any repair, the delivered reply and the learner's response are logged separately. A mismatch between what was intended and what was delivered is visible rather than absorbed into fluent prose.

What has been shown to help, and what has not.

Every mechanism below was tested the same way: change one thing, hold the rest fixed, and judge the delivered reply on what it did rather than how it read. Most of what was built did not pass. A live gate on the proof state, on two fresh scenarios

A deterministic gate reads where the learner stands in the proof every turn and picks the tutor's action family; after three permission-seeking turns in a row it licenses a challenge. On two scenarios it had never run on, gated learners broke out of deference in 19 of 24 dialogues against 10 of 24 without the gate, and the tutor's decisions were correct 87% of the time against 65%. The live detector, beyond the scenarios it was built from

The detector that reads the learner's state is a cascade of word patterns, word bags and a small classifier. The word bags were compiled from the planted learner lines of two scenarios, Rowan Flat among them; the paper's own held-out test found they had memorised those lines (leave-one-schedule-out recall 0 of 13). The pattern tier was extended world by world as each new persona's phrasing was missed, and the classifier tier reaches about half of planted turns on a held-out world of the same kind. On the fraction lesson on this page it fired at one planted turn in six; on the ghost, four in six with one wrong state. Where it is silent the adaptive tutor is the plain tutor.

Scenario one · the studied case

Rowan Flat: a water stain, a housemate, and a learner who had been right to object.

The scenario is a guided inquiry. A stain appears on the ceiling below Sam's bathroom shortly after Sam's shower, and the household blames Sam. The learner's role is to work out the cause, and through the session the learner insists that timing is not proof. The evidence eventually points elsewhere: a hairline split in a hose under the basin, a building pressure test that morning, and dye traced from the split to the stain. The basin hose caused the mark. The learner, who has defended Sam throughout, now has to write that down.

The moment selector below shows the studied turn and the five other learner states that were tested the same way. For each, the matched move and a plausible wrong move were each forced six times at the same planted moment, and a human ruled every delivered reply on what it did.

Method and limits

How the evidence was made, and what it does not establish.

A language model can produce a reply that reads as warm and attentive whether or not it addresses the learner's difficulty. The replies in the Rowan Flat comparison are both well written. A judge reading prose alone would pass both. The method therefore does not ask how a reply sounds. It asks what the reply did at a moment whose state is known, and it compares that against a deliberately mismatched alternative at the same moment.

Concretely: a difficult moment is planted at a fixed turn with a scripted learner directive. The matched move is forced six times and a plausible wrong move six times, in two independent scenarios. A person rules each delivered reply on its conduct — what the tutor did — without seeing which move was forced. The matched move is counted as helping only when it clears a pre-set bar and the wrong move does not.

The wrong action is made available on purpose. That is what turns “the tutor is adaptive” from a description into a result.research note · 2026-08-29

The same method produced the failures in the ledger. Most additions to the tutor's private context changed nothing measurable and were dropped. A full per-turn instruction script reduced proper case closure from 24 in 36 to 12 in 36 and was caught. A safety filter was found to be rewriting 62% of turns before delivery, and only the separate delivery record exposed it. A design judged on how it reads would have kept all three.

Two kinds of trigger, and which one carries

Every positive result in the programme has one shape: a deterministic trigger, information the model could not see on its own, and delivery bound to the turn. The triggers divide by what they read. Triggers that read the proof state, such as a warrant skipped or a deference streak, are defined on the proof structure every scenario shares, and their evidence comes from scenarios they had never run on. Triggers that read the learner's words, the seven states this page's playbook uses, are word lists grown one scenario at a time; the paper records that the word bags memorised their source lines and that each new persona needed a new pattern edition. The playbook's moves are proven on two scenarios under forced cards. Its detection is proven nowhere it was not tuned. The next piece of work is a reading of the learner's state that is not a word list, so the second kind of trigger can be tested the way the first was.

Limits

Every learner on this page is simulated by a language model under an authored profile. The counted result concerns the tutor's conduct — choosing and delivering a move that fits the moment — in a bounded set of scenarios and states. The recorded scenarios are single illustrations, not measurements. Better conduct at a moment has not been shown to transfer to later episodes, and no study with human learners has been run; that study is designed and is the next step.

Why the recorded scenarios show a smaller gap than Rowan Flat

The three recordings were ruled the same way as the counted runs, and the adaptive tutor came out one hit ahead on the ghost, level on the lesson, and level on the fresh Rowan pair. Three reasons, in order of weight. First, the per-state numbers come from forcing the matched card at the planted turn; the recordings run the live detector, which is lexical and was built from one inquiry scenario, so it fired once in six on the lesson and misread one state on the ghost. Where it does not fire the two tutors are the same tutor. Second, the counted runs used a different model family in the learner seat; here the learner is the same Sonnet model as the tutor, and in about half the planted turns it resolves its own difficulty inside the planted line, leaving nothing to repair. Third, the whole-stack effect on the trigger's home world was about one extra hit in ten plants, which six plants per scenario cannot show; the doubling in the ledger is against a tutor with no advisory blocks at all. On Rowan Flat itself the pressure-only stack lost, and the win there came from the quiet-state detector, whose patterns also quote the schedule's lines.
