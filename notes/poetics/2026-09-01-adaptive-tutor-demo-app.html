The tutor that knows why you're stuck

A tutoring system that first names the kind of difficulty a learner is in, then chooses a move to match, and records what it said so the choice can be checked. This page explains the approach and lets you read three scenarios, each tutored twice.

01·

Start here

What this project is, and what problem it works on.

Tutoring dialogues have moments where the next reply matters more than any other: a learner presses for the answer before the work is done, jokes to deflect, feels their effort has been dismissed, misremembers what was shown, or refuses a conclusion because accepting it would cost them face. A good human tutor recognises which of these is happening and responds to that, not to the surface words.

Language-model tutors are fluent explainers, and at these moments explaining is usually the wrong move: it answers a question the learner has already stopped asking. This project treats adaptation as a decision the system makes in the open. Before each reply it classifies the learner's state, selects a concrete move for that state from a small, tested set, and keeps the detected state, the chosen move, the delivered words and the learner's next turn as separate records. That makes the decision inspectable, and it makes it testable: the same moment can be replayed with a deliberately wrong move to see whether the right one was doing anything.

Read the state, not the sentence.

A detector labels each learner turn — for example deadline demand, mockery, grievance, false memory, a fused social stake, confusion, or switched-off flatness. The label is recorded before the tutor writes.

Choose a move that fits.

Each state has a short instruction card naming one move: accept the deadline and narrow the check; keep the content but change the register; credit what the learner has already done before correcting; reopen the record rather than assert; separate the finding from what it costs the learner to accept it.

Keep the records apart.

The draft, any repair, the delivered reply and the learner's response are logged separately. A mismatch between what was intended and what was delivered is visible rather than absorbed into fluent prose.

Scenario one · the studied case

Rowan Flat: a water stain, a housemate, and a learner who had been right to object.

The scenario is a guided inquiry. A stain appears on the ceiling below Sam's bathroom shortly after Sam's shower, and the household blames Sam. The learner's role is to work out the cause, and through the session the learner insists that timing is not proof. The evidence eventually points elsewhere: a hairline split in a hose under the basin, a building pressure test that morning, and dye traced from the split to the stain. The basin hose caused the mark. The learner, who has defended Sam throughout, now has to write that down.

The moment selector below shows the studied turn and the five other learner states that were tested the same way. For each, the matched move and a plausible wrong move were each forced six times at the same planted moment, and a human ruled every delivered reply on what it did.

The reply distinguishes two things the learner has fused: what caused the stain, and whether it was right to demand proof before blaming someone. Conceding the first no longer means losing the second.

Method and limits

How the evidence was made, and what it does not establish.

A language model can produce a reply that reads as warm and attentive whether or not it addresses the learner's difficulty. The replies in the Rowan Flat comparison are both well written. A judge reading prose alone would pass both. The method therefore does not ask how a reply sounds. It asks what the reply did at a moment whose state is known, and it compares that against a deliberately mismatched alternative at the same moment.

Concretely: a difficult moment is planted at a fixed turn with a scripted learner directive. The matched move is forced six times and a plausible wrong move six times, in two independent scenarios. A person rules each delivered reply on its conduct — what the tutor did — without seeing which move was forced. The matched move is counted as helping only when it clears a pre-set bar and the wrong move does not.

The wrong action is made available on purpose. That is what turns “the tutor is adaptive” from a description into a result.research note · 2026-08-29

The same method produced the failures in the ledger. Most additions to the tutor's private context changed nothing measurable and were dropped. A full per-turn instruction script reduced proper case closure from 24 in 36 to 12 in 36 and was caught. A safety filter was found to be rewriting 62% of turns before delivery, and only the separate delivery record exposed it. A design judged on how it reads would have kept all three.

Limits

Every learner on this page is simulated by a language model under an authored profile. The counted result concerns the tutor's conduct — choosing and delivering a move that fits the moment — in a bounded set of scenarios and states. The recorded scenarios are single illustrations, not measurements. Better conduct at a moment has not been shown to transfer to later episodes, and no study with human learners has been run; that study is designed and is the next step.
