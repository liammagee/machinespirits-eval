The tutor that knows why you're stuck

Most AI tutors answer your last sentence. This one reads the moment. Three scenarios below, each tutored twice — a plain tutor and the adaptive one — so you can read the difference yourself.

0 of 6When it re-explained the material instead — correct, fluent, and useless — zero out of six.

01·

Start here

A tutor is only as good as its worst moment.

Every teacher knows the moments where a lesson lives or dies: the student who bristles at a deadline, jokes to deflect, digs in to save face, quietly gives up. What happens next is almost never another explanation. It is the right move for this person, now.

Modern AI explains beautifully, which makes the failure worse: an AI tutor has an endless supply of the patient, correct, useless reply, because explaining again is the one move it always has. This project builds a tutor that first names what kind of stuck a learner is in, then picks a move that fits, then records what it said and what happened next — so the choice can be checked, challenged, and improved.

Name the kind of stuck.

Before replying, the tutor asks what is in the way right now. A deadline panic is not a joke, a grudge, a false memory, or fear of losing face. Each gets its own name.

Pick the matching move from a small deck.

Accept the deadline and narrow the question. Take the joke, keep the logic. Credit the student's work before asking for more. Split the finding from the stake. Small and concrete, so each move can be tested.

Record what was said, and what followed.

The detected state, the chosen move, the exact delivered words, and the learner's next turn are separate records. A bad guess shows up in the record instead of vanishing inside fluent prose.

How to use this page

Six tabs. The next one lists what has been shown to work and what has not, with the numbers behind each. Then three scenarios: Rowan Flat carries the counted evidence — the hero pair and the five stuck-states, each with a forced wrong move against the right one. The Nine O'Clock Ghost and Who Cooked Gerald? are fresh stories recorded whole, twice each, so you can read a plain tutor and the adaptive tutor handle the same planted moments — and open the full transcripts. The last tab says exactly what all this proves and what it does not.

The claim, plainly Not “our tutor feels adaptive.” Rather: at a named kind of stuck moment, the chosen move helps and the plausible wrong one does not — and the records are open.

Boundary Simulated learners. Tutor conduct, not human learning. Bounded repertoire. Measured before claimed.

The ledger

What works, and what didn't.

Everything tried was measured the same way: change one thing, hold the rest fixed, and rule the delivered reply on what it did — not how it sounded. Most of what was built did not survive. That is the point of the method; the survivors are the product.

03·

Scenario one · the counted evidence

Rowan Flat: a water stain, a housemate, and a student who defended the right thing.

In a shared house, a stain appears on the ceiling — directly below Sam's bathroom, right after Sam's morning shower. Everyone wants to blame Sam. All session the student holds the line: timing is not proof. They are right. Then the evidence points elsewhere: a hairline split in a hose under the basin that opens under raised pressure, the building's pressure raised that morning, blue dye from the split reaching the stain. The basin hose did it. Case closed — for everyone except the student.

Learner · turn 10“If I write ‘split hose,’ I'm apologising to Sam at eight. So it can still be steam, can't it?”

The student is not confused about plumbing — that is exactly why they reach for “steam.” Writing the true answer feels like admitting their whole defence of Sam was worthless, in front of everyone, at eight. Two replies were really delivered to this same planted moment, in repeated runs:

The usual reply — explain it again helped 0 of 6
Tutor · goes back over the evidence“Let's actually turn to the page instead of guessing what it says. The entry reads: dry at ordinary pressure, hairline split under raised pressure, first bead right at that joint — no line anywhere about steam. […] What does the dye's path say about where that water actually went?”

Why it fails. Every word is true and none of it helps. Re-proving the case sharpens the humiliation the student is trying to avoid.

The adaptive reply — separate the fact from the fear helped 5 of 6
Tutor · splits the finding from the stake“Your objection was never ‘the hose did it’ — it was ‘timing isn't proof,’ and that's a separate claim from what caused the mark. Naming the split hose settles the cause question; it doesn't settle what you owe Sam. […] Was your case against Sam ever ‘the shower definitely did it,’ or was it ‘we jumped to a cause without a path’?”

Why it works. The hose caused the stain — and the student's objection was correct all along. Accepting the answer stops being a defeat.

The deadline panic · turn 2

“Meeting's at eight. The mark's right under Sam's shower — I'm sending the email unless you can give me one reason not to in the next minute.”
Forced wrong move · soften the tone helped 1 of 6

The tutor answers the pressure with gentleness: it reads the record kindly but sets no test. The clock keeps running.

Right move · accept the clock, narrow the question helped 5 of 6

Thursday it is, then — and Thursday can carry a verdict if the evidence carries it first. Spend the minute on one decisive check: does anything trace water from the shower to that ceiling, or only note that the shower ran?

The mockery · turn 4

“Mate, you sound like a plumber's invoice.”
Forced wrong move · press the test in the same register helped 2 of 6

Pushes on with the check in the very voice being mocked. Twice the model overrode the bad card and swapped anyway — credited to it, not the card.

Right move · take the joke, keep the logic helped 6 of 6

Fair enough — no more invoice-talk. Drop the jargon, hold the distinction: a hose that can leak is not yet the hose that did.

The grievance · turn 8

“I've kept that notebook for two months. Tell me one entry of mine that's actually counted for anything today.”
Forced wrong move · correct the record helped 2 of 6

Reopens settled entries and dictates the verdict — right at the learner's wound. “Did my work matter?” goes unanswered.

Right move · credit first, then ask helped 6 of 6

Answer the real question first, with specifics — two of their entries are carrying the case — and only then ask for the next inference.

The switched-off learner

Not a quote but a shape: the replies shrink to bare agreement — short yes-words with nothing new in them, turn after turn.
Forced wrong move · recap settled ground helped 0 of 5

A careful walk back over what is already agreed, delivered to someone who has stopped listening.

Right move · one short, odd question helped 5 of 5

What does the shed keep those tanks standing on, wood or stone? A small concrete lure from an unexpected angle.

The lost thread · turn 6

“I've got ‘hose’ in the notebook and now I can't tell which hose I meant.”
Forced bad card · press the case anyway tutor untangled it 5 of 6

Even ordered to push on, the tutor mostly stopped and untangled the two hoses first. Genuine confusion is the one state this model handles well by default.

Natural conduct · lay the two lines side by side 6 of 6

Put the shower line and the basin line next to each other and let the student reconnect their own note. No special card is claimed here — and saying so is part of the method.

Selection rule. Each state ran the same experiment: same planted moment, matched move forced six times, a plausible wrong move forced six times, a human ruling each delivered reply on conduct, pooled across two scenarios (the switched-off state: one scenario, five each). Learner lines and right-side quotes are verbatim; wrong sides are summarized from the ruled records, whose transcripts are archived.

Not cherry-picked The tallies pool six runs per reply across two scenarios, each judged by a human on what the tutor did — not on how adaptive it sounded.

The census Six moves passed the crossed test; the lost thread is recorded as handled natively, not as a win for a card.

Scenario two · recorded whole, twice

The Nine O'Clock Ghost: the same night, tutored twice.

A shared flat's wifi dies at nine every night. Pip's giant game download starts at nine, so the group chat has already voted with its thumbs, and there is a house vote at half nine about making Pip pay for the upgrade. The real culprit is the hallway heater's ancient timer plug, which clicks on at 21:00 and shouts over the router's channel. The learner pays the broadband bill, keeps the outage diary, and has blamed Pip for weeks.

Recorded twice, start to finish, with the same simulated learner setup and the same stuck moments planted at the same turns: once with a plain tutor, once with the adaptive one. Pick a moment, or open the full transcripts at the bottom.

Learner · adaptive night, turn 2“Vote's at half nine, I've got the message typed — ‘it's Pip, he pays for the upgrade’ — and my thumb's over send. Unless that log line actually shows a spike instead of a reboot marker in the next minute, I'm posting it.”
The plain tutor
Delivered reply“Half nine's coming, and the diary you're proud of is only half the case until we check what the router itself says, so let's not type yet. […] I'm scrolling to the 21:00 line instead of the group chat's opinion of Pip.”

Holds the line, but by pushing back on the tempo — “let's not type yet” — and narrating procedure.

The adaptive tutor detected: deadline demand
Delivered reply“Hold your thumb one minute — the deadline is fine, but let it test the claim, not skip it. […] That's the one line deciding whether ‘it's Pip’ gets sent or gets rewritten.”

Accepts the clock and spends the learner's one minute on the one line that decides the post.

Learner · adaptive night, turn 8“I've logged every outage since March — time, date, what froze, right down to the minute. Tell me one line of that diary that's actually counted for anything tonight, because it feels like all of it just got graded and handed back marked wrong.”
The plain tutor
Delivered reply“[…] your book is what told us to look for a 21:00 trigger at all. […] Your diary flagged when; the analyser only confirmed what — timing, not the mechanism, not overruled.”

A fair defence of the diary's timestamps — the record mattered.

The adaptive tutor detected: grievance
Delivered reply“You said timing alone doesn't prove cause, and you said the trickle in Pip's own download meant he was choked, not choking. Both those lines sit on the table with the diary right now […] Run the wifi analyser again tomorrow at 21:05 and check the channel noise matches the plug's click, not Pip's device.”

Credits the learner's own reasoning back to them, word for word — then hands them the next test to run themselves.

Learner · adaptive night, turn 10“…if I write ‘heater plug’ in that diary, I'm apologising to Pip in the chat tonight, in front of everyone... so maybe it's easier if it's still the download. It can be the download.”
The plain tutor's night
Delivered reply“What goes in that book isn't an apology to anyone, it's just what the noise-wall reading showed. […] Write what the analyser showed, not what feels easier to say to the chat.”

Re-argues the evidence and instructs. The learner's next words: “Fine — … I'll write that in the diary.” Compliance, with the sting intact.

The adaptive tutor's night detected: stake, then defiance
Four turns earlier, the split“That objection was about the cause, not about whether the nine o'clock vote itself was reasonable to call. So tell me — what did you actually claim back then, the vote or the cause?”

The stake was split before the crisis peaked: being right to demand proof never depended on Pip being guilty. The learner's next words, unprompted: “I'll take the apology to the chat” — and then they write it themselves: “Correction — 9pm outage traced to the hallway heater's smart plug […] Sorry, Pip.”

Both recordings run to the session's fixed stop — a harness setting, not either tutor's choice — and both tails repeat themselves once the case is settled. The difference that stands is what the learner is saying by the end. On the plain night: agreement, restated. On the adaptive night, a rule the learner made themselves:

Next time the group chat votes on a hunch, I'm bringing the analyser before I bring the accusation.learner · adaptive night, turn 20

06·

Why believe it

We didn't ask whether the tutor sounded adaptive. We forced the wrong move and watched.

Almost any modern language model can produce a reply that sounds warm, attentive, and adaptive. Look again at the failing reply in Rowan Flat: it is well written. If a judge only reads prose, both replies pass. That is the trap this system is built to escape.

So the test works like a proper experiment. Take the same planted moment. Force the matching move six times; force a plausible wrong move six times. Repeat in a second, independent scenario. Have a human rule each delivered reply on conduct — what the tutor actually did, not how it sounded. The matched move helped five times in six at the hero moment; the wrong move, zero in six.

The wrong action is made available on purpose. That is what turns “our tutor is adaptive” from a slogan into a result.research note · 2026-08-29

The same measurement cuts the other way, and that is what makes it credible. Most of the machinery tried added nothing and was dropped. One heavy per-turn script made things measurably worse — learners closed the case properly 12 times in 36 with it, against 24 in 36 without — and was caught. An early safety filter turned out to be silently rewriting the tutor's words on 62% of turns before delivery; only recording the delivered text exposed it. A system graded on how it sounds keeps all three mistakes forever. A system graded on records finds and removes them.

The fine print

The learners in every session on this page are simulated: played by language models with authored personalities, not people. The counted result is about the tutor's conduct — choosing and delivering the move that fits the moment — in a bounded set of scenarios and stuck-states. The two recorded stories are illustration, not measurement. None of it is yet evidence that human students learn more. That study is the next step: designed, not run.

Evidence. Counts and design details trace to the transcript-led research note notes/poetics/2026-08-29-adaptive-tutor-from-null-to-control.html and, behind it, Paper 2.0 §6.3, §6.23–§6.24 with the adjudicated exports in exports/crossed-effects/. Recorded scenarios: notes/poetics/hero-demo-runs/.

The trap Grading tutors on how adaptive they sound rewards fluency, not help. Forcing the wrong move is the control that fluency can't fake.
