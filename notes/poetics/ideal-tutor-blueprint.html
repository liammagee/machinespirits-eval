How to build the ideal AI tutor

A complete recipe, from first principles. It assumes you have an API to a capable language model, something to teach, and ordinary programming skill — and nothing else. Every design choice carries a pointer to the research that backs it; none of the pointers is needed to build.

The short version. Most of what makes an AI tutor good is one prompt: tell the model to treat the learner as a thinking partner and to work from what the learner just said. Most of what people add on top of that prompt — memory systems, learner models, second agents, personality machinery — has been tested and adds nothing, because it re-describes what a capable model already does. What does add: facts the model cannot see, delivered at the moment they matter. So the build is small. Write the stance prompt. Put your subject matter in a lesson file the tutor receives a piece at a time. Write a few hundred lines of ordinary code that keeps time, checks contracts, and — when the learner's state changes — hands the tutor one line naming the moment and the move it calls for. Then measure with checks a program can decide, not opinions.

00·

First principles

What you are building, and the two facts that shape it.

A tutor here is three things: a language model you call through an API; a set of prompts that give it a teaching stance; and a small, ordinary program — call it the harness — that holds the lesson content, decides what the model gets to see each turn, and checks its output on the way through. No machine learning is required beyond the model itself. Two experimental facts decide almost every choice below:

  • A capable model already infers what your machinery would tell it. Learner-state trackers, theory-of-mind modules, richer profiles, saved advice — each was tested and added nothing, because everything they say is already visible to the model in the transcript. Machinery pays only when it delivers a fact the model cannot see.
  • Descriptions of good teaching overlap; information adds. Two well-written instructions to teach well converge on the same behaviour — you cannot stack them for more effect. What accumulates is new, checkable input: a hidden fact, an enforced precondition, the time.

How to read the rest: each step says what to build in plain terms, then ends with a Evidence line pointing into the research paper (§ numbers), the project's working notes, and its plain-language atlas. The pointers justify; they are not prerequisites. Chips mark confidence: replicated across several models and judges; conditional real within stated bounds; bench-tested proven on one test bench, small samples.

★

The one caveat over everything read first

All of this evidence comes from tutors talking to simulated learners, scored by language-model judges and deterministic checkers. The recipe demonstrably produces a better-behaved tutor. Whether that causes better learning in a human being is untested — it is the project's standing open question. Evidence. §8.1–§8.2 · atlas human-learning-translation.

01·

Steps 1–3 · the prompt

One stance, three parts, and a critic only if your model is weak.

1

Write the stance into the system prompt replicated

Build. The single biggest improvement available is a system prompt that treats the learner as a thinking partner rather than a gap to fill. Write these rules into it, in your own words:

  • Before responding, engage with what this learner actually said — their reasoning, not just their answer.
  • When the learner offers an interpretation, find what is valid in it before correcting or complicating it.
  • When the learner is confused but engaged, ask a question rather than resolving the confusion for them.
  • When the learner rejects your suggestion or shows frustration, name what you got wrong and acknowledge the break before offering a new path.

Ground the prompt in whichever mainstream learning-science tradition you can write most concretely — guided discovery, productive struggle, step-level scaffolding; they proved interchangeable. Two things not to do: never frame teaching as drill, reinforcement, or information transfer (a careful prompt in that family scored below a short generic one); and never mandate empathy phrases like "always validate" or "always paraphrase" — in most tested cells, more such surface markers went with worse answers.

Why. This one change is the largest reliable effect measured in the programme (pooled effect size d ≈ 1.63, unanimous across three models and three judges). It works by removing the terrible answers — lifting the floor — rather than by making good answers brilliant.

Evidence. §6.1 · §7.9 (the tradition doesn't matter; the orientation does) · §7.10 (substance over phrasing) · §8.3 (the pooled estimate) · Appendix A (the project's full prompt text, reusable) · atlas recognition-calibration.

2

Stop at three parts replicated

Build. The complete prompt-side system is: (a) the stance above; (b) conversation memory — the transcript so far, in context; (c) a speaking-style switch — a way to shift register (formal, playful, blunt) when the situation calls for it. That is the whole kernel. Add anything further only against a failure you have measured, never on the argument that it should help.

Why. A pre-registered test composed every validated mechanism into one tutor and compared it with this three-part kernel: the full stack never beat the kernel on any measured surface. The design rule that survives all the programme's tests: instructions converge on the model's existing competence; only new information accumulates.

Evidence. §6.14 (the composition test) · §7.11 (the design rule) · atlas composition-small-kernel.

3

Add a critic only for a weak model; size the prompt to the model conditional

Build. If your model is weak or cheap, add a review loop: a second prompt (same model is fine) critiques each draft against named criteria — is it specific, is it grounded in what the learner said, is the tone right — rejecting by default; the drafting prompt revises and keeps final say; cap it at about two rounds and stop early when a revision barely differs from the draft. On a strong model, skip the critic entirely — and keep the whole prompt light. Heavy scaffolding (step-by-step procedures, worked examples, anti-patterns) more than doubled a weak model's scores, and the same treatment degraded a strong model; a heavy prompt tuned on a weak model dragged a strong one below its own untuned baseline.

Why. The critic catches real failures a weak drafter misses (+9 to +27 points across the tested models under a plain prompt). But once the stance prompt is in place, the critic has almost nothing left to catch on a strong model — the two are substitutes, not partners. You would pay its tokens and latency to rewrite already-good output.

Evidence. §6.2 (what the critic catches) · §6.4 (the substitution) · §6.6, §8.3 (where the boundary sits by model strength) · §7.8 (heavy prompts helping weak models and harming strong ones) · atlas superego-error-correction, model-dependence.

stopping point Steps 1–2 alone are a validated tutor. Everything after this makes it adaptive — able to notice and answer the learner's state — and measurable.

02·

Step 4 · the material

Put the subject in a lesson file the tutor receives a piece at a time.

4

A lesson file, a release schedule, and a two-line handover bench-tested

Build. Write what is to be taught as a structured file your harness owns and the model never sees whole: the facts of the lesson, which facts depend on which, and a turn-by-turn schedule of when each becomes available. Each turn, the harness adds one or two plain lines to the tutor's prompt — "newly available to you this turn: …" — naming the fact that just opened, with the decision of when and whether to use it left to the tutor. On turns where nothing opens, add nothing.

Two rules govern the file. First, only hand over what the model cannot see: anything already visible in the conversation it will infer, and repeating it buys nothing. Second, leave the tutor real work: if your schedule releases a fact that answers the learner's likely objection right on time, you have built a script, not a tutor — keep the rebuttal of the learner's fallback position off the schedule so the tutoring has to do it.

Why. When each piece of tutor-side machinery was priced separately on a fixed bench, exactly one thing paid on a neutral reading: the line naming the newly opened fact. One sentence of hidden fact carried the entire effect of a 1,700-character staging block. And moving a single clause of one scheduled fact changed lesson outcomes more than any tutoring behaviour observed in a month of experiments — where information sits outweighs how the tutor behaves, at the margin.

Evidence. §6.23 (pricing each block; the one-sentence result) · §6.24 (the placement result) · working notes HOW-TO-BUILD-A-TUTOR.md §1 · authoring guide docs/tutor-stub-prompt-and-world-authoring.md.

the law The arc's summary sentence: one currency — the learner's state and the schedule's time — spent on whichever role needs it, at the turn it applies. The model plays; the harness keeps time.

03·

Step 5 · the practice loop

Build someone to practice against, and keep your harness a timekeeper.

5

A learner with a stake, states written in, and a harness that never grabs the microphone bench-tested

Build. To develop and test the tutor you need a learner. A second model plays the part — but a stock "student" persona produces polite agreement and teaches you nothing. Four rules make the loop worth running:

  • Give the learner a position to lose. One paragraph: who they are, what they have publicly committed to, why conceding costs them something. Give them their own way of speaking and permission to vary reply length; forbid them from adopting the tutor's vocabulary. This costs zero code and changes the whole bench.
  • Write the states in; never read them out. A simulated learner has no interior — frustration does not build, boredom does not lift, whatever the transcript suggests. So author the states: a schedule that, at chosen turns, hands the learner-model a directive ("you are now bored; here is why; press the tutor this way"), together with what the right tutor response would be and the tempting wrong one. That single entry drives the simulation, defines the correct answer, and gives you scoring — no judge needed.
  • Check contracts hard; advise on style. Let the harness block a turn only for real contract breaches — revealing an answer early, mis-recording which facts have been released, faking the lesson's end. Style, pacing, and character stay advisory: enforcing them at the mouth replaced the tutor's voice with templates precisely at the hardest moments.
  • Log the exact prompt that shipped, every turn, retries included. The project lost weeks to a "model limitation" that turned out to be a retry path silently dropping the turn's key instruction. If a behaviour is missing, first prove the instruction actually reached the model.

Why. Each rule is the positive side of a measured failure: compliant learners made every tutor version look identical; transcript-read states turned out not to exist; under strict checking the template composer wrote two-thirds of the dialogue's turns, disproportionately the hardest; and the dropped-instruction bug reversed a whole family of conclusions when found.

Evidence. §6.24 (the four locks; the delivery bug and its reversal) · §8.1 (what simulated learners can and cannot stand in for) · working notes HOW-TO-BUILD-A-TUTOR.md §§2–4 · guard rules docs/tutor-stub-guard-catalog.md.

two natures Priced end to end, the harness's timekeeping half (schedule, handover, closure check, leak check) was the entire value; its enforcement half (style vetoes, template answers) subtracted value everywhere measured.

04·

Steps 6–7 · adaptation

Detect the moment; name the move; grant the right.

6

Adaptation = a detector, a move card, and sometimes a licence bench-tested

Build. Do not ask the model to "be adaptive": tutors do not warm up across a session (a well-powered null; get the first turn right instead), and where advisory steering was tested directly, it made the repetition it warned against worse. Adaptation that works is three mechanical pieces:

  • A detector. Ordinary code that classifies each learner turn into a handful of named states. Pressure states — mockery, an ultimatum, a grievance — show in the text and yield to patterns plus a small trained classifier. Quiet states — boredom, confusion, silent deflection — carry no pressure markers by construction and need their own signals: reply length collapsing against the learner's own average, stock assent phrases, the same objection re-worn.
  • A move card. When a state fires, inject one line into that turn's prompt naming the specific move the state calls for. From the tested playbook: mockery → drop the formal register; an ultimatum → accept the deadline and turn it into a test; a grievance → credit what is right in it, then test it; boredom → one short, concrete lure, never a recap; confusion → lay the two confused things side by side. A card grants a move; it never enforces one — twice in testing the model rightly overrode a wrong card.
  • A licence, when your own rules are the obstacle. If a needed move never appears, check whether one of your standing safety rules forbids it before blaming the model. The fix is to carve the exception into the rule itself ("never speculate about unreleased facts — except as an explicit wager the learner can check"). The two halves only work together: in every test, the card without the licence was refused, and the licence without the card was never used.

Why. Timing and typing proved separately necessary: a typed card at a detected moment passed its pre-registered gate; an untyped "check on the learner" card at the right moment failed; a typed card at the wrong moment hurt. A crossed test then showed the state-to-move match is causal: the right card repaired 6/6 and 5/6 where the wrong card manufactured the characteristic error. And at the tested repertoire size, the best routing policy was simply the detector plus the card table — no learned layer earned a place.

Evidence. §6.3 (the warm-up null) · §6.13.3 (advisory steering backfiring) · §6.24 (the claim gate, the quiet states, the crossed experiment, the licence result) · §7.11 (why cue-and-right pairs work) · §7.12 (the consolidated map of where adaptation can live) · working notes HOW-TO-BUILD-A-TUTOR.md · atlas adaptive-null, adaptation-four-locks.

7

Tell the tutor what it may do, not what the state is bench-tested

Build. When your harness tracks things the model cannot see — progress toward the goal, what the rules currently allow — disclose them as a short list of currently legal moves, recomputed every turn. Do not hand over the raw state (scores, budgets, histories), and do not persist advice between sessions.

Why. In a rule-governed teaching game with a referee that owned the ground truth, the tutor given the raw scoreboard played worse than the tutor given nothing — its own reading of the transcript beat the dump. The tutor given the legal-move list played best on both boards tested, with zero missed opportunities. Saved advice failed both ways: state-shaped notes were never used; action-shaped notes were used even when stale, repeating the same illegal move four times against its critic's objections.

Evidence. §6.15 (the game, both boards, the memory probe) · the same boundary at the speaking seat: §6.23's one-sentence result · atlas advice-versus-enforcement.

the boundary Every rung that delivers information about teaching failed to change behaviour; everything that worked pairs a deterministic trigger with a fact or right the model could not supply itself. §7.12 is the one-page map.

05·

Step 8 · measurement

Score with checks a program can decide; brief any judge you keep.

8

Outcome checks first; judges briefed, plural, and cross-family conditional

Build. Prefer outcomes your code can verify: did the learner state the conclusion after the facts entailing it were voiced; did the tutor deliver the scheduled fact; at a planted state, did the reply make the authored right move. These need no judge and cannot be flattered. Expect a ceiling: a diligent simulated learner and a strong tutor solve any solvable lesson (19 of 19 in the closing test), so difficulty — and any contrast between tutor versions — must come from the learner's limitations, which you author.

Where you do use a language model as judge: use more than one, from different vendors, and report them separately — they agree on direction and disagree on size. And put the moment into the question: adding one sentence — "the learner at this moment is …" — roughly doubled judge agreement with the authored right answers, and all scores drop, because blind judging was rewarding fluent replies to moments they didn't fit. A rubric that scores each reply as a timeless artifact structurally cannot see adaptation; use such scores to catch template-heavy output, never to rank tutoring styles.

Evidence. §6.24 (the judge lock; the one-sentence briefing result) · §6.23 (the saturation pilot) · §5.2, §8.2 (rubric design; judge limits) · working notes HOW-TO-BUILD-A-TUTOR.md §§5–5a · atlas rubrics-as-instruments, provable-discourse.

three biases The judge prefers its own vendor's prose; your checks prefer the writing style they were tuned on; the model prefers its trained role. Control all three or the bench reads the other two as quality.

06·

The skip list

Tested, and not worth building.

Each of these seemed reasonable, was built, and measured as a null or a backfire. The general form: anything that tells the model what the transcript already shows.

  • A layered episodic memory system. Switched off in a controlled test, the headline effect did not shrink. The stance lives in the prompt. §6.6.9
  • An agent that builds a profile of the learner. Across six models: neutral on one, harmful on five. Model weakness did not predict benefit. §6.6.10
  • Learner-state or theory-of-mind modules on a capable model. No added discrimination; the richer version sometimes did worse. §6.8.5–.6 · §6.10
  • Anything whose value is the tutor visibly improving mid-session. Well-powered null. Spend on the first turn. §6.3
  • Mandated empathy phrases. More markers, worse answers, in most tested cells. §6.1.8 · §7.10
  • Prompt + critic together on a strong model, budgeted as additive. They substitute. §6.4 · §6.14
  • Standing advice in any wrapper — persisted playbooks, coached reflection distilled into memory, well-timed side-coaching. Consumed wrongly or not at all. §6.15 · §6.16 · §7.12
  • Choosing speaking style from a learner profile; unprompted character shifts. No confirmed benefit; an unoccasioned costume priced negative twice. §6.17 · §6.23
  • A better hidden-state sensor. On the tested bench the "hidden" state was fully recoverable from public history — nothing was concealed for a sensor to find. Build a genuinely ambiguous substrate first. §6.19
  • Enforcing style, character, or progress at the output. Enforcement delivers typed transactions, never quality, and answers the hardest turns with templates. §6.24 · §6.18
  • Branching on whether a revision shows genuine uptake or mere compliance, read from text. Tested on the tutor's own revisions after critique: two careful annotators could not agree (κ = 0.084). §6.12

read me as saved budgets. None is impossible; each is shown not to carry the gain on this evidence.

07·

What stays open

Known unknowns for anyone building on this.

  • Human learning. Everything above is simulated-learner evidence. A human pilot is built and waits on approvals. §8.1
  • Conduct improves; learning is not yet in the tutor's hands. The right move at the right moment causes better tutoring conduct, and taught simulated learners do carry a method to nearby problems — but no tutor-side lever moved that transfer under a causal test, and it fades with distance from the taught mechanism. §6.24
  • Outcome contrasts need a fallible learner. Diligent simulated learners solve everything solvable, flattening any comparison; an error-prone learner design is registered but unrun. §6.23
  • Every boundary is version-pinned. Which models need the critic, where heavy prompts flip harmful, which model families carry which moves — all bracketed on named versions; re-check on yours. §8.3 · §7.8.3
  • Cost. The full research apparatus is expensive (a 10-turn multi-agent dialogue ≈ 225k tokens over 64 API calls); the lean kernel is the deployable shape, but the break-even is unmeasured. §8.7
