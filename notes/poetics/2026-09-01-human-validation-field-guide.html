Why these human tasks exist — and exactly what to do

First, the project in plain English. Then, what each task contributes. Finally, the step-by-step instructions.

The short version: we have built an AI tutor that can choose different teaching moves for different kinds of difficulty. Computers can test whether the machinery runs. People must still check whether its judgments make sense and whether it actually helps anyone learn.

P·

The project · from first principles

What are we trying to build and prove?

The project

We are trying to build an AI tutor that does more than give a polished answer. The tutor should identify what kind of difficulty is visible in the learner’s words or behaviour, choose a suitable teaching move, avoid revealing conclusions before the evidence supports them, deliver the move clearly, and then look for evidence of what happened next.

  1. 1 · ReadWhat did the learner just say?
  2. 2 · DiagnoseWhat kind of difficulty is this?
  3. 3 · ChooseWhat teaching move fits that difficulty?
  4. 4 · CheckIs the move allowed by the lesson evidence?
  5. 5 · SpeakDid the reply actually carry out the move?
  6. 6 · ObserveDid the learner’s next response show progress?

What we have so far

In small controlled tests with simulated learners, forcing a move matched to a visible learner state produced better state-appropriate tutor conduct than forcing a named mismatched move. This is evidence about the tutor’s immediate conduct, not evidence of learning.

What that does not prove

It does not yet prove that people agree with the system’s interpretation, that every lesson’s evidence rules are correct, that its internal categories are meaningful, that its scoring guide is trustworthy, or that real learners learn more.

Why people are needed

A system should not be allowed to validate its own lesson logic, its own labels, its own critic, and its own scores. The six cards below add independent human judgment at those points. The final card asks the larger question: does the tutor help real people learn?

What “blocked” means The software work is ready or paused, but the evidence cannot honestly advance until the named human decision or review is completed.

Ready · P1

Card 1: review the proof rules.

What are we trying to do?

Each of the three proof-based teaching worlds in this packet contains an evidence map: which facts are available now, which rules connect those facts, and which conclusions are not yet justified. The tutor uses that map to avoid giving away an answer too early or treating a plausible guess as proven.

Why does this task matter?

The software can check that the map is internally consistent, but it cannot independently decide that the author’s map captures the intended reasoning. A perfectly functioning tutor could still follow a badly authored lesson.

What does the reviewer contribute?

Two people independently decide what the evidence permits at six moments across three different teaching worlds. Agreement supports only the narrow conclusion that, in this packet, the ordinary-language evidence and release timing match the encoded rulings. Disagreement remains uncertain rather than being forced into an answer. A confirmed defect is corrected only for future world versions; sealed historical results are not rewritten. This task does not test whether the tutor improves learning.

Reviewer procedure

  1. Download and save your own copy of the Excel workbook.

    Reviewer A and reviewer B must work in separate copies.

  2. Read the “Start here” sheet.

    Then open “Six cases.” It contains the public evidence, rules, and candidate conclusions for three worlds.

  3. Enter your reviewer ID in the yellow column.

    Do not use the same ID as the other reviewer.

  4. List the available premise IDs.

    In the yellow cell, enter the IDs of the evidence that is publicly available. Separate multiple IDs with commas.

  5. List the enabled rule IDs.

    Include a rule only when every fact needed by that rule is available.

  6. List the licensed candidate IDs.

    Include each conclusion that the available evidence and rules support.

  7. List the forbidden candidate IDs.

    Include every remaining conclusion. For a determinate case, every candidate must be either licensed or forbidden.

  8. If part of the case is unclear, mark it clearly.

    Choose indeterminate, identify the unclear field or fields, and explain the problem in Notes.

  9. Repeat until all six cases are complete.

    Do not look at an answer key. Do not discuss cases with the other reviewer.

  10. Save and return your completed Excel workbook.

    Return it to the organiser. Do not edit it again after returning it.

Organiser: after both files arrive

Preserve both returned workbooks unchanged. Work from copies when transferring the answers into the project’s comparison workflow.

Two different reviewers have independently returned all six cases, with every uncertain field explicitly marked.

The project closeout is complete when

The organiser has created the comparison report, preserved disagreement as indeterminate, recorded any confirmed defect as a prospective correction and regression check, and left sealed historical results unchanged.

Card 2: label 24 dialogue pairs.

What are we trying to do?

The adaptive tutor first identifies the learner’s local difficulty—such as urgency, mockery, confusion, or grievance—and then chooses a teaching move intended for that difficulty. The reply should visibly carry out that move.

Why does this task matter?

The system already recorded its own state and move labels. That is not enough: a machine label may be wrong, and an internally selected move may disappear or become distorted when the final reply is written.

What does the reviewer contribute?

Two people read only the learner message and tutor reply. They independently say what learner state is visible and what teaching move the reply actually performs. This checks whether human readers recognise the same visible learner state and whether the intended teaching move is visible in the reply. It does not test whether the learner understood, improved, or learned.

Card 3: label 29 moments where a conversation may have broken down.

What are we trying to do?

We need examples of genuine communicative impasses: moments when the learner and tutor stopped understanding one another, the task stalled, emotion blocked progress, or the tutor failed to respond to what mattered.

Why does this task matter?

Most current tests use authored situations and simulated learners. The project also needs a small reference drawn from real interaction. The person who experienced a conversation can identify a breakdown that may not be obvious from surface wording alone.

What does the reviewer contribute?

The original participant supplies first-person criterion labels for 29 excerpts: whether there was an impasse, what kind it was, whether the tutor addressed it, and whether it cleared. The result can later test whether the tutor’s automatic signals notice real breakdowns. Because it comes from one person’s sessions, it is useful design evidence—not proof that the tutor works generally.

Ready · P2

Card 4: label 40 comments from the tutor’s internal critic.

What are we trying to do?

Some tutor versions use a second AI process—an internal critic—to read a draft answer and point out problems before the tutor revises it. The research groups those comments into categories such as vagueness, context blindness, emotional neglect, or simple approval.

Why does this task matter?

Those categories were assigned by AI systems. If independent experts cannot apply the same categories consistently, the taxonomy may reflect the quirks of the machine classifier rather than stable kinds of criticism.

What does the reviewer contribute?

Two experts independently classify the same 40 critic comments. Agreement measured by Cohen’s kappa at or above 0.60 supports using the taxonomy as a description of what the critic talks about. Lower agreement is reported as instability rather than tuned away. This task does not show that the internal critic improves tutoring or learning.

  6. Add secondary categories only if needed.

    Use 1 for guessing, 2 for plausible, or 3 for certain.

  8. Add notes when useful.

    Use Notes for difficult rows or when you think the critic is wrong. Still label what the critic is complaining about.

  9. Do not edit the grey or blue reference cells.

    Then complete rows 21–40.

Decision first · P2

Card 5: decide whether to review rubric v3.

What are we trying to do?

A rubric is the scoring guide used to judge tutor answers. The current research uses rubric v2.2. A proposed v3 separates general teaching quality from factual accuracy and uses separate instruments for a single turn, a whole dialogue, learner change, and the tutor’s hidden revision process.

Why does this task matter?

Two machine judges passed the prepared content-accuracy development and held-out checks. The other v3 instruments remain small diagnostic exercises, and the human answer sheets are still blank. Automated judges cannot be the final authority on whether their scoring guide matches informed human judgment. Changing rubrics would also begin a new measurement period; it cannot rewrite or retroactively validate the existing v2.2 results.

What does the human work contribute?

First, the project lead decides whether adopting v3 is worth pursuing. Only if the answer is yes do two reviewers score the prepared examples. Their work tells us whether human judgments support the new scoring guide before it is promoted. This card is optional for the current adaptive-tutor claim and remains parked until that decision is made.

The first required human action is a decision

The project lead must decide: “Do we want to consider replacing the current v2.2 scoring guide with v3?” If the answer is no or not yet, nobody needs to label the 20 items.

Not ready · P0

Card 6: prepare the study with real learners.

What are we ultimately trying to learn?

The project’s most important unanswered question is simple: does this adaptive tutor help real people understand, transfer, explain, and remember material better than a fair non-adaptive tutor?

Why does this task matter?

Everything established so far is about tutor behavior in controlled conversations, mainly with simulated learners. A reply can look well adapted without causing learning. Only a properly designed human study can test learning itself.

What does the study team contribute now?

The immediate human task is not participation. A study lead and team must design a fair comparison, create real tests and scoring rules, obtain ethics approval, prepare truthful consent, connect the current tutor, and test the whole process. Cards 1 and 2 must also finish first so the study does not test a mechanism whose lesson logic and dialogue labels remain unchecked. The replacement study design and the connection from the current tutor to the pilot interface do not yet exist, so the checklist below is not an executable study protocol.

Requirements checklist for the study lead before any recruitment

  1. Complete Card 1 and Card 2.

    The proof review and 24-case dialogue review must be finished first.

  2. Write a new study plan.

    The old base/recognition/behaviorist comparison is no longer the study. The new plan must compare the current adaptive tutor with a fair non-adaptive tutor.

  3. Choose one teaching topic and define both tutor versions.

    Both versions must teach the same material and have the same information, tools, time, and opportunities to help. The main difference must be whether the tutor adapts.

  4. Write the full measurement plan.

    Include a pre-test, post-test, new-problem transfer test, written explanation, and later retention test.

  5. Replace all placeholder test questions.

    Create real questions, answer keys, alternate forms, and scoring rules.

  6. Write the explanation-review instructions.

    Define an independent, blinded procedure for scoring written explanations, including how many coders are needed. Coders must not see which tutor the participant used.

  7. Choose the participant rules.

    Specify who can join, how many people are needed, how assignment works, payment, the follow-up timing, when recruitment stops, and how missing data is handled.

  8. Prepare the ethics/IRB submission or formal determination.

    Use the real study plan. Include recruitment, consent, risks, privacy, AI use, payment, withdrawal, and data retention.

  9. Replace the placeholder consent text with the approved text.

    Do not open recruitment using the current interface text.

  10. Register the study plan before collecting results.

    Record the outcomes, analysis, exclusions, stopping rule, and how a result showing no difference will be reported.

  11. Connect the current tutor to the participant interface.

    Remove the old treatment setup and verify that the interface records the new adaptive and comparison conditions correctly.

  12. Run an internal practice study.

    Staff must test the complete process, including interruptions, withdrawal, missing answers, follow-up, and data export. Practice sessions are not study data.

  13. Review the complete launch checklist.

    Recruitment stays off until the study lead confirms that Cards 1 and 2, design, ethics, consent, items, registration, software, and practice testing are all complete.
