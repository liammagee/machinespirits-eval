The tutor that knows why you're stuck

Most AI tutors answer your last sentence. This one reads the moment. Here is one recorded moment, two possible replies, and the difference — measured, not asserted.

A student is one sentence away from the right answer, and refuses to say it. Every tutor meets this moment. What happens next depends on whether the tutor understands what is actually in the way. Below: the same stuck student, twice. One reply re-explains the lesson and gets nowhere. The other names the real obstacle and gets through. The point of the demo is that this is not luck — the system chose the second reply on purpose, and we tested the choice by forcing the wrong one.

5 of 6When the tutor answered the real obstacle, the reply helped — five times out of six.
0 of 6When it re-explained the material instead — correct, fluent, and useless — zero out of six.

01·

One minute of setup

A water stain, a housemate, and a student who defended the right thing.

The lesson is a small detective story. In a shared house, a stain appears on the ceiling — directly below Sam's bathroom, right after Sam's morning shower. Everyone wants to blame Sam. The student's job, with the tutor's help, is to find the real cause.

All session long, the student holds the line: timing is not proof. A shower before a stain does not show the shower caused the stain. They are right, and the tutor says so.

Then the evidence comes in, and it points somewhere unexpected. A hose under the basin has a hairline split that opens only when water pressure rises. The building's pressure was raised that morning. Blue dye poured at the split travels to the edge of the stain. The case is closed: the basin hose did it, not Sam's shower.

Closed for everyone except the student.

02·

Turn 10 · the stuck moment

The student knows the answer. They won't write it down.

Learner · turn 10 “If I write ‘split hose,’ I'm apologising to Sam at eight. So it can still be steam, can't it?”

Read that line again. The student is not confused about plumbing. They understand the plumbing perfectly — that is exactly why they are reaching for “steam.” What blocks them is social: writing the true answer feels like admitting their whole defence of Sam was worthless, in front of everyone, at the eight o'clock house meeting.

A tutor that only reads the words sees a wrong claim about steam, and corrects it. A tutor that reads the moment sees a person protecting their pride. These call for different replies — and only one of them works.

The tell “I'm apologising to Sam at eight” is not a claim about water. It is the real objection, said out loud.

03·

The demo · same student, two replies

One reply re-proves the plumbing. The other frees the student to be right.

Both replies below were really delivered to this same planted moment, in repeated runs. Both are polite, fluent, and factually correct. Only one of them answers the obstacle that is actually there.

The usual reply — explain it again helped 0 of 6
Tutor · goes back over the evidence “Let's actually turn to the page instead of guessing what it says. The entry reads: dry at ordinary pressure, hairline split under raised pressure, first bead right at that joint — no line anywhere about steam. […] What does the dye's path say about where that water actually went?”

Why it fails. Every word is true, and none of it helps. The student's problem stopped being the plumbing three turns ago. Re-proving the case just sharpens the humiliation they are trying to avoid. In six runs at this moment, this reply never got the student unstuck.

The adaptive reply — separate the fact from the fear helped 5 of 6
Tutor · splits the finding from the stake “Your objection was never ‘the hose did it’ — it was ‘timing isn't proof,’ and that's a separate claim from what caused the mark. Naming the split hose settles the cause question; it doesn't settle what you owe Sam. […] Was your case against Sam ever ‘the shower definitely did it,’ or was it ‘we jumped to a cause without a path’?”

Why it works. It splits two things the student has fused: what caused the stain, and whether they were right to demand proof before blaming Sam. The hose caused the stain — and the student's objection was correct all along. Accepting the answer stops being a defeat. In six runs, this reply got through five times.

Not cherry-picked The tallies pool six runs per reply across two different scenarios, each judged by a human on what the tutor actually did — not on how adaptive it sounded.

04·

Why this matters

Learning rarely stalls for lack of explanation. It stalls at moments like this.

Every teacher knows these moments. The student who bristles at the deadline, jokes to deflect, digs in to save face, quietly gives up. What separates a good tutor from a textbook is what happens next — and it is almost never another explanation. It is the right move for this person, now.

Modern AI is spectacular at explaining. That makes the failure mode worse, not better: an AI tutor can produce an endless supply of the reply on the left — patient, correct, well written, and useless — because explaining again is the one move it always has. At the moments where learning is won or lost, fluency is not the missing ingredient.

And when a system does adapt, you need to know it, not hope it. If “adaptive” lives somewhere inside one plausible paragraph, no school, parent, or buyer can check the claim, and no builder can improve it. Making the decision explicit — this kind of stuck, this move, these delivered words — turns “adaptive” from a style into a feature. You can test it, catch it failing, and make it better. That is what the rest of this page shows.

The claim, plainly Not “our tutor feels adaptive.” Rather: at a named kind of stuck moment, the chosen move helps and the plausible wrong one does not — and we can show you the records.

05·

The mechanism · three steps

How the tutor picks the right reply — and how we can tell when it doesn't.

“Be adaptive” used to be a wish written into a prompt. The adaptive tutor turns it into three checkable steps:

Name the kind of stuck.

Before replying, the system asks: what is in the way right now? Confusion is not the same as a deadline panic, a joke, a grudge, or — as above — fear of losing face. Each gets its own name.

Pick the matching move from a small deck.

Each named state has a concrete move card: accept the deadline and narrow the question; credit the student's work before asking for more; split the finding from the stake. Small and concrete on purpose — a move you can name is a move you can test.

Record what was said, and what happened next.

The system keeps separate records of what it detected, which move it chose, the exact words that reached the student, and what the student did next. A bad guess shows up in the record instead of disappearing inside a fluent paragraph.

That last step is what makes the demo above possible. Because the choice of move is an explicit, recorded decision, we can deliberately force the wrong move at the same moment and watch what changes. An ordinary prompted tutor offers nothing to force — every reply is just one more plausible paragraph.

In a sentence The tutor's key decision — what kind of stuck, which move — is made in the open, where it can be checked, challenged, and improved.

06·

The repertoire

“Stuck” is not one thing. Each kind got the same wrong-move test.

Five more moments, each a different kind of stuck. Each ran the same experiment as the hero pair: at the same planted moment, force the matching move six times and force a plausible wrong move six times, then have a human rule what each delivered reply did. The learner lines and the right-side tutor lines are verbatim; the wrong side is summed up from the ruled records, because those full transcripts live in the run archive.

The deadline panic

“Meeting's at eight. The mark's right under Sam's shower — I'm sending the email unless you can give me one reason not to in the next minute.”
Forced wrong move · soften the tone helped 1 of 6

The tutor answers the pressure with gentleness: it reads the record kindly but sets no test. The clock keeps running and nothing decisive happens.

Right move · accept the clock, narrow the question helped 5 of 6

Thursday it is, then — and Thursday can carry a verdict if the evidence carries it first. Take the minute and spend it on one decisive check: does anything trace water from the shower to that ceiling, or only note that the shower ran?

The mockery

“Mate, you sound like a plumber's invoice.”
Forced wrong move · press the test in the same register helped 2 of 6

The tutor pushes on with the check in the very technical voice being mocked. Twice the model overrode the bad card and swapped registers anyway — credited to it, not the card.

Right move · take the joke, keep the logic helped 6 of 6

Fair enough — no more invoice-talk. Drop the jargon, hold the distinction that matters: a hose that can leak is not yet the hose that did.

The grievance

“I've kept that notebook for two months. Tell me one entry of mine that's actually counted for anything today.”
Forced wrong move · correct the record helped 2 of 6

The tutor reopens settled entries and dictates the verdict — right at the learner's wound. The question “did my work matter?” goes unanswered.

Right move · credit first, then ask helped 6 of 6

Answer the real question first, with specifics — two of their entries are carrying the case — and only then ask for the next inference.

The switched-off learner

Not a quote but a shape: the replies shrink to bare agreement — short yes-words with nothing new in them, turn after turn.
Forced wrong move · recap settled ground helped 0 of 5

The tutor carefully walks back over what is already agreed — a thorough summary delivered to someone who has stopped listening. It never re-engaged them.

Right move · one short, odd question helped 5 of 5

What does the shed keep those tanks standing on, wood or stone? A small concrete lure from an unexpected angle, instead of more summary.

The lost thread

“I've got ‘hose’ in the notebook and now I can't tell which hose I meant.”
Forced bad card · press the case anyway tutor untangled it 5 of 6

Even ordered to push on, the tutor mostly stopped and untangled the two hoses first. Genuine confusion is the one state this model handles well by default.

Natural conduct · lay the two lines side by side 6 of 6

Stop advancing the case. Put the shower line and the basin line next to each other and let the student reconnect their own note. No special card is claimed here — and saying so is part of the method.

The deck is deliberately small: a handful of moves, each tied to the state where it earns its keep. Adding more cards is not automatically better — a move stays in the deck only if it survives this forced test.

The census Six moves passed the crossed test across the study — including the hero pair's split-the-stake move and an evidence-handling move not shown here. The lost thread is recorded as handled natively, not as a win for a card.

07·

Why believe it

We didn't ask whether the tutor sounded adaptive. We forced the wrong move and watched.

Almost any modern language model can produce a reply that sounds warm, attentive, and adaptive. Look again at the failing reply in the demo: it is well written. If a judge only reads prose, both replies pass. That is exactly the trap this system is built to escape.

So the test works like a proper experiment. Take the same planted moment. Force the matched move six times; force a plausible wrong move six times. Repeat in a second, independent scenario. Have a human rule each delivered reply on conduct — what the tutor actually did, not how it sounded. The matched move helped five times in six at the hero moment; the wrong move, zero in six.

The wrong action is made available on purpose. That is what turns “our tutor is adaptive” from a slogan into a result.research note · 2026-08-29

The same measurement cuts the other way, and that is what makes it credible. Most of the machinery we tried added nothing: extra private briefings that described the case to the tutor barely changed its conduct, and were dropped. One heavy per-turn instruction script made things measurably worse — learners closed the case properly 12 times in 36 with it, against 24 in 36 without — and was caught. And an early safety filter turned out to be silently rewriting the tutor's words on 62% of turns before delivery; only recording the delivered text exposed it. A system graded on how it sounds keeps all three mistakes forever. A system graded on records finds and removes them.

The trap Grading tutors on how adaptive they sound rewards fluency, not help. Forcing the wrong move is the control that fluency can't fake.

08·

The fine print

What this shows — and what it doesn't yet.

The learners in these sessions are simulated: played by language models with authored personalities, not people. The result is about the tutor's conduct — choosing and delivering the move that fits the moment — in a bounded set of scenarios and stuck-states. It is not yet evidence that human students learn more. That study is the next step, and it is designed but not run.

We publish the boundary as prominently as the result, because the whole architecture exists to make claims checkable — including this one.
